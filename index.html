About Me

Organic Chemist Eager to Explore
Multidisciplinary Research

I am an organic chemist eager to explore multidisciplinary research for impactful outcomes, with a deep specialisation in carbohydrate chemistry and biomimetic catalysis. My research sits at a compelling intersection — using the structural complexity of glycals and their rearrangements to access pharmacologically relevant scaffolds, while simultaneously designing metal-ligand systems that replicate the enzymatic elegance of nature.

At Pondicherry University under Prof. J. Kandasamy, I developed an efficient synthetic route to D-Rhamnal from D-Glucal and exploited it to synthesise novel C-2 aryl glycosides via Pd-catalysed arylation — work now being prepared for peer-reviewed publication. My Ferrier-I rearrangement studies produced 2,3-unsaturated glycosides in yields exceeding 95%, establishing robust methodology across four glycal precursors.

I am currently a Junior Research Fellow (JRF) at NIT Calicut under Dr. M. Sankaralingam, exploring biomimetic analogues of Lytic Polysaccharide Monooxygenases (LPMOs) — copper-dependent enzymes central to sustainable biomass valorisation. I seek a European PhD position at the frontier of synthetic methodology, green catalysis, and chemical biology.

The Work, in Detail

Each project described as a scientific narrative — the question, my specific contribution, and what emerged.

Nature-inspired Conversion of Biomass to Biofuel: The LPMO Paradigm
Feb 2026 — Present
🏛 National Institute of Technology Calicut, India
Project Associate (JRF) · Guide: Dr. M. Sankaralingam, Associate Professor
Scientific Question
"Can we design synthetic molecular systems and functional nanomaterials that replicate the oxidative C–H activation achieved by copper-dependent LPMOs in nature?"
Lytic Polysaccharide Monooxygenases (LPMOs) are copper metalloenzymes that perform the remarkable feat of oxidatively cleaving recalcitrant crystalline cellulose — a bond inert to most conventional reagents. My role is twofold: (1) design and synthesise biomimetic ligand scaffolds that support Cu-coordination environments analogous to the LPMO active site; and (2) tune surface chemistry of nanomaterials and MOFs to replicate LPMO reactivity in heterogeneous systems.
Currently synthesising first-generation ligand candidates and characterising Cu complexes by UV-Vis, EPR, and cyclic voltammetry to probe redox behaviour.

Glycochemistry Research Programme — Ferrier Rearrangements, D-Rhamnal Synthesis & C-2 Aryl Glycoside Development
July 2024 – May 2025
🏛 Pondicherry University, India
Internship → M.Sc. Thesis (10 months, continuous) · Guide: Prof. Dr. J. Kandasamy, Associate Professor, Dept. of Chemistry
Central Scientific Question
"Can a systematic Ferrier-I methodology across structurally diverse glycals be leveraged as the entry point for an efficient D-Rhamnal synthesis — and can that rhamnal be further functionalised via Pd-catalysed C-2 arylation to access novel, pharmaceutically relevant C-aryl glycosides?"
The entire programme was driven by a single connected logic: build a robust glycal methodology first, then exploit it to access chemically significant targets. In the first phase (internship, July–Dec 2024), I systematically benchmarked the Ferrier Type-I rearrangement across four glycal precursors — D-glucal, D-galactal, D-rhamnal, and L-rhamnal — optimising conditions for each substrate and establishing a reproducible, high-yielding protocol. Yields exceeded 95% across all substrates, and this methodological groundwork directly set the stage for what followed. Alongside the experimental work, I conducted a comprehensive critical literature review (25+ publications) analysing the evolution of the Ferrier reaction from classical BF₃·OEt₂-mediated conditions through modern green alternatives — ionic liquids, Amberlyst, zeolites, and photocatalytic systems — evaluating each against atom economy, E-factor, selectivity, and scalability. This critical reading was not peripheral; it was what identified the methodological gap that the thesis stage was designed to address.

In the second phase (thesis, Jan–May 2025), building directly on this foundation, I developed an efficient synthetic route to D-Rhamnal from D-Glucal through selective C-6 hydroxyl modification and C-5 configuration control — a precursor route significantly shorter than existing literature methods. With D-Rhamnal in hand, I deployed it as a pseudoglycal substrate in a palladium-catalysed C-2 arylation protocol under ambient conditions, contributing to the synthesis of a range of structurally diverse C-2 arylated substrates across different pyranose frameworks. The work systematically explored aryl coupling partners, solvent conditions, and catalyst loading to map the scope of the transformation. However, the protocol revealed genuine challenges: substrate stereochemistry proved to be a significant limiting factor, with anomeric selectivity varying substantially across different glycal precursors and aryl groups, making predictable stereocontrol difficult to achieve consistently. Additionally, protecting group effects exerted a pronounced influence on the reaction outcome — certain protecting group combinations on the glycal hydroxyl positions either suppressed reactivity entirely or led to undesired elimination and migration side products. These drawbacks, honestly encountered and carefully documented, clarified the boundaries of this Pd-arylation protocol and directly define the questions I intend to pursue at the PhD level — particularly the design of catalytic systems and substrate architectures that can overcome these stereochemical and electronic limitations.
D-Rhamnal synthesised via an efficient novel route; diverse C-2 arylated substrates accessed under ambient conditions — stereochemical limitations and protecting group sensitivity identified, documented, and set as open questions for future PhD-level investigation.

Research Vision

The Problems I Want to Solve

My PhD goal — in the words a professor needs to hear in the first 60 seconds.

"Two of the most pressing challenges in modern synthetic chemistry converge precisely at the boundary of my expertise. First: the carbohydrate chemical space — arguably the most structurally complex and biologically essential class of natural products — remains extraordinarily difficult to access synthetically. Despite a century of glycochemistry, the selective construction of C-glycosidic bonds, particularly with control over anomeric configuration and C-2 functionality, continues to require lengthy routes incompatible with rapid analogue synthesis in drug discovery. My Ferrier-arylation work has given me a direct window into this problem, and I believe new C–H and C–M activation strategies applied to glycal platforms offer genuinely unexplored solutions.

Second: the enzyme family of LPMOs has revealed that nature solves the problem of activating inert C–H bonds in polysaccharides using elegant copper coordination chemistry — a feat synthetic chemists have only begun to replicate. My JRF work at NIT Calicut positions me at this exact frontier. My PhD ambition is to design the next generation of biomimetic Cu catalysts that bridge the mechanistic precision of the LPMO active site with the synthetic accessibility demanded by scalable biomass valorisation."

Prof. Seeberger's automated glycan assembly platform directly addresses the accessibility problem I encountered in my own glycal chemistry. Prof. Jeroen D. C. Codée — Leiden University, Netherlands
Glycan synthesis · conformational analysis · stereoselective glycosylation
The Codée group's mechanistic rigour in understanding anomeric selectivity, neighbouring-group participation, and the conformational dynamics of glycosyl donors closely mirrors the stereochemical questions I encountered in my C-2 arylation work. Their computational/experimental approach to glycosylation mechanism is something I want to contribute to and learn from.
Prof. Bert Sels — KU Leuven, Belgium
Sustainable catalysis · biomass valorisation · heterogeneous catalysis for lignocellulosic conversion
Prof. Sels's group works precisely at the interface of catalysis and biomass conversion that defines my JRF project — their integration of material science, mechanistic chemistry, and sustainability metrics is a model I aspire to follow. A PhD here would allow me to extend my LPMO-mimicry work into real heterogeneous systems with direct bio-refinery impact.
